Homework 3: SVIX and the Probability of a Crash#

  • 01. E-mini S&P 500 Options Data from Databento

  • 03. The Daily Monitor

  • Launched: week 5. Due: Tuesday, November 10, at the start of the week 7 session, two weeks after it launches. Also posted on Canvas.

  • Link to Assignment: a private copy is created for you in the hw-finm-32800 organization, named hw3- followed by your GitHub username. The copies are created in the week of October 27. If you have accepted the invitation to the organization, yours simply appears there.

  • Goes with: weeks 4 and 5

Overview#

Two replications and one deployment.

Ian Martin’s SVIX index measures the variance the options market expects for the S&P 500, the way the VIX does but weighting every strike equally, and the same option prices give the probability that the market falls 20% within a month. Martin publishes both series on his website, and CME computes the same construction as its CVOL family of volatility indexes for its interest-rate, currency, energy, metals, and agricultural options. The first replication rebuilds both numbers from the settlement prices of E-mini S&P 500 options on CME Globex, pulled from Databento, sets SVIX next to the Cboe VIX, and checks the SVIX integral against Martin’s own published series.

The deployment is the point of the assignment. The CME FedWatch tool you rebuilt in HW 2 updates itself every day; your replication did not. This time it will: a GitHub Actions workflow runs your pipeline every morning, pulls the previous session, adds it to a history kept on a branch of its own, and republishes your ChartBook site to GitHub Pages, with your Databento key stored as a secret. That is continuous deployment, and the autograder that has scored your pushes since HW 1 is the same machinery.

The second replication is offline. Martin and Shi (2025) extend the crash probability to single stocks, with bounds on the probability that Apple or AIG falls 20%, built from the OptionMetrics volatility surface on WRDS. You complete the query that pulls the surface and run their chain.

As in HW 1 and HW 2, everything runs as a pipeline and the unit tests are the specification. New this time: the pipeline is run with uv. uv run doit installs Python 3.12 and the locked dependencies into .venv/ the first time and runs the pipeline; there is no environment to create or activate. The same command runs on the GitHub Actions runner. Run uv run pytest, read the first failing test, and write the code that passes it. Do not edit the test files.

The assignment has four parts, A to D, worth 19 points in total, one per test. Every push to your private copy runs the suite on the course runner and posts your score to the repository’s Actions tab. Search the repository for TODO to find the blanks.

Budget your time. Parts A, B, and D are a few hours together. Part C is where the hours go, because every run of the workflow takes about ten minutes and the first ones fail. Finish Part B and run uv run doit locally until it is green before you touch Part C.

Before you start#

  • uv. Install it once from docs.astral.sh/uv (one command on macOS, Linux, or Windows). Then, in the repository, uv run doit. In VS Code, select the interpreter in .venv/. If you would rather keep working in the finm conda environment, requirements.txt is exported from the lockfile and pip install -r requirements.txt gives the same versions.

  • Your Databento API key in .env, as in HW 2, and later as a GitHub secret. Everything this assignment pulls is on the CME Globex feed and free under the course license; the pull checks the cost before downloading anything.

  • Your WRDS username in .env, for Part D. The OptionMetrics pull is about a minute.

  • A public repository in your own GitHub account, for Part C. The workflow needs free Actions minutes and GitHub Pages, which private repositories in the course organization do not have. The README explains how to push the same code to both.

Part A (graded, 2 pts): GitHub Skills#

Complete two tutorials from the GitHub Skills page, using public repositories in your own GitHub account, and record the links in src/github_skills.py as in HW 1 and HW 2.

  • Hello GitHub Actions: write and run your first workflow. Part C asks you to write a real one.

  • Introduction to secret scanning: what GitHub does when a key reaches a public repository anyway. In Part C your Databento key goes into a public repository’s secret store, and it must never go anywhere else.

Part B (graded, 5 pts): SVIX and the probability of a crash#

From one session’s settlement prices for the two end-of-month expirations that bracket 30 days. The pull, the parity forward, the implied volatilities, and the smile on a fine grid are written for you. Three formulas are not:

  • E-mini S&P 500 Options Data from Databento

  • Replicating SVIX and the Probability of a Crash

The blanks, each a formula the second guide derives:

  • SVIX² (src/svix.py). The simple variance swap rate is an integral of out-of-the-money option prices across strikes, Martin’s equation (12). Two tests: a flat smile, where the answer has a closed form, and Martin’s published SVIX, below.

  • The risk-neutral distribution (src/rnd.py). The slope of the call curve in the strike is the risk-neutral CDF of the return, Breeden and Litzenberger’s result. The isotonic fix that follows it is given. Two tests: the result is a valid CDF, and a flat smile gives the lognormal.

  • The log investor’s crash probability (src/crash_prob.py). Martin turns the risk-neutral probability of a 20% drop into the belief of an investor who holds the market, with one more integral of the same distribution. One test against the closed form.

Four of the five tests run offline on synthetic smiles and need no data.

Does the replication match? Martin publishes his one-month SVIX for 1996 to 2012. The fifth test runs your SVIX formula on the OptionMetrics S&P 500 surface at every month-end in that period and requires it to stay within a fraction of a vol point of his series, so it needs the OptionMetrics pull from Part D. No exchange publishes an SVIX for the S&P 500: CME’s CVOL indexes use the same construction, but not for equity options. The fourth guide shows the comparison day by day, and the second guide sets SVIX next to the VIX.

Part C (graded, 8 pts): the daily monitor#

  • The Daily Monitor

Four things to complete, then one to do.

  • The daily update (dodo.py, 1 pt). task_latest pulls the latest session, adds its row to _output/svix_monitor_history.csv if the history does not have it, and redraws the chart. Unlike every other task it must run every time it is invoked. Plain uv run doit is the backfill: it pulls every session in the window in the settings, the trailing week unless you set START_DATE earlier, and adds the ones the history does not have.

  • The ChartBook manifest (chartbook.toml, 1 pt). Register the monitor notebook and the history dataframe.

  • The workflow (.github/workflows/deploy_pages.yml, 5 pts). The file is given as a skeleton with five blanks: the schedule, the secret, the permission the job needs to push, the step that runs the pipeline, and the branch the site is deployed to. The tests read the YAML. The other steps are written for you; read their comments. One restores the history from the monitor-history branch and another records the day’s row there, so no output ever lands on main; a workflow that pushed to main on every run would be a workflow that triggers itself. Another tells a data-source outage from a bug: if Databento or FRED is still down after the pull’s retries, the run ends with a warning and yesterday’s site stays up. See Building a Live Monitor with GitHub Actions, cron, and the github_actions/ examples in the in-class repository, which deploy a daily pipeline with a Databento secret the same way.

  • The live site (src/published_site.py, 1 pt). Push the completed repository to a public repository in your own account, add your Databento key as a repository secret, run the deploy workflow once from the Actions tab, run the backfill workflow once with a start date so the chart has a history behind it, turn on GitHub Pages from the gh-pages branch, and record the address of the site and of the repository. The steps are in the README. Your private copy in the course organization keeps getting your pushes for grading; the workflow does not run there.

When it works, the chart on your site gains one point every morning.

Part D (graded, 4 pts): single names, from the OptionMetrics surface#

  • Crash Bounds for Single Names (Martin and Shi 2025) (optional reading). Part D needs two of its sections: The data, which describes the surface query, and SVIX against Martin’s published series, the check of Part B’s fifth test.

Martin and Shi (2025) bound the probability that a single stock crashes using the risk-neutral distributions of the stock and of the market. Their code, from two of last summer’s students, is given; so are the pulls of prices and rates. The blank:

  • The surface query (src/pull_optionmetrics.py). Complete the WHERE clause that selects the S&P 500, Apple, and AIG at the 30- and 91-day maturities over the sample window from the OptionMetrics surface table. Two tests run the query against a small in-memory database with the WRDS layout, as HW 1 tested its queries.

Then uv run doit single_names pulls the surface and runs the chain, with your Breeden-Litzenberger step from Part B inside it. The third test checks that the chain ran across the sample, and the fourth runs the index probability from the surface against the monthly series Martin publishes, on the 1996 to 2012 overlap.

Does the replication match? The fourth guide shows Apple’s and AIG’s bounds next to the paper’s Figure 1, and our index probability next to Martin’s series.

Credits#

The engine that recovers the risk-neutral distribution and computes the bounds is the work of Riley Haas and Marija Jovicic, from their Summer 2026 replication of Martin and Shi, used with their permission. The SVIX integral follows Andrew Heekin and Chandler Bird’s Summer 2026 replication of Martin (2017).
